Similarity is everything

Netflix picking your next movie and ChatGPT picking its next word come down to the same question: what is closest to this? This week we turn taste into numbers and measure how close two people are.

The hostel movie problem

Four friends in your wing have rated four movies, from 1 (hated it) to 5 (loved it). On Friday, Aarav asks: "What should I watch next?"

You could just tell him the most popular movie. But Kabir loves sci-fi and Aarav doesn't, so "most popular" may be wrong for Aarav. A better idea is to find the friend whose taste is most like Aarav's and borrow their opinion.

That one idea is the core of recommendation systems. To use it we need two things:

1
A way to write taste as numbers (a vector).
2
A way to measure how close two vectors are.
In weeks 3 and 4 the same two ideas come back for words. An LLM turns every word into a vector and asks which words are close. So this week is the foundation for both halves of the project.

By the end of this week you can

  • Compute distance and cosine similarity, first by hand and then in NumPy
  • Build two recommenders: "most popular" and "friend most like you"

Taste becomes a list of numbers

Write each friend's ratings in the same movie order. Each row is now a vector, which is just an ordered list of numbers. Aarav is [5, 4, 1, 1].

Notice two things before any maths:

  • Aarav and Meera like the same movies and rate them in a similar way.
  • Zoya likes the same movies as Aarav but is a strict rater. Her "love" is a 3, not a 5.

A good similarity measure should say Zoya is like Aarav. Keep that in mind. It's the twist in the next tab.

Why only 2 numbers in the picture above?

We can't draw 4 dimensions. So the picture at the top squeezes each friend into 2 numbers: their average rating for the two drama-comedies, and their average for the two sci-fi movies. The maths works the same for 4 numbers, or for 4,000.

Two ways to measure "close"

Way 1: distance (a ruler)

distance(a, b) = √( (a₁−b₁)² + (a₂−b₂)² + … )
  • Subtract each pair of ratings, square the gaps, add them up, then take the square root.
  • Small distance = close. 0 means identical.

Aarav vs Meera: gaps are (5−4, 4−5, 1−1, 1−2) = (1, −1, 0, −1). Squared: 1+1+0+1 = 3. √3 ≈ 1.73.

Aarav vs Zoya: gaps (2, 2, 0, 0). Squared: 4+4 = 8. √8 ≈ 2.83. The ruler says Zoya is further away, only because she gives lower scores.

Way 2: cosine similarity (a compass)

cos(a, b) = (a · b) / ( |a| × |b| )
  • a · b ("a dot b"): multiply matching ratings and add. Aarav·Zoya = 5×3 + 4×2 + 1×1 + 1×1 = 25.
  • |a| ("length of a"): √(sum of squares). |Aarav| = √(25+16+1+1) = √43 ≈ 6.56. |Zoya| = √15 ≈ 3.87.
  • Divide: 25 ÷ (6.56 × 3.87) ≈ 0.98.
  • It only cares about direction, not length. 1 = same direction, 0 = nothing in common.
The compass ignores how generous someone is and only asks whether they point the same way. That's why Zoya wins under cosine.

Your first two recommenders

A new movie, Jawan, is out. Meera, Kabir and Zoya have seen it. Aarav hasn't. Should we recommend it to him?

Recommender A: most popular

Average each movie's rating across everyone, then show the top one to everyone.

The problem: Kabir rated 3 Idiots a 1, yet he'd get it as his top pick. The same list goes to everyone, so it's personal to no one.

Recommender B: friends like you

Predict Aarav's rating as a weighted average: friends whose taste is closer to his (higher cosine) get more say.

guess = Σ (similarity × their rating) / Σ similarity
  • Σ means "add up over all friends who saw it".

Puzzle for the discussion session: Zoya gave Jawan a 3. Coming from her, is that low or high? Her average is only 1.75. Next week we fix this by comparing each rating with the person's own average, which is called mean-centering.

Sneak peek: words are vectors too

Score each word on two made-up features: "is it a fruit?" and "is it a tech company?". Each word becomes an arrow, just like each friend did.

Apple sits between the two groups, because it's both a fruit and a company. A plain word vector can't tell which one you mean.

LLMs fix this by looking at the context, the words around it. "I ate an apple" pushes the arrow toward fruit. "Apple's new phone" pushes it toward tech. That trick is called attention, and it's week 3.

Feature scores are made up to show the idea. Real models learn hundreds of features nobody names.

The same thing in Python

Everything above in about 20 lines. Run it in Colab and check that the numbers match the tables.

# Week 1 starter: similarity between friends
import numpy as np
import pandas as pd

# Each row is a friend, each column a movie (ratings 1-5)
ratings = pd.DataFrame(
    [[5, 4, 1, 1],
     [4, 5, 1, 2],
     [1, 1, 5, 4],
     [3, 2, 1, 1]],
    index=["Aarav", "Meera", "Kabir", "Zoya"],
    columns=["3 Idiots", "Dangal", "Interstellar", "Inception"])

def cosine(a, b):
    # dot product: multiply matching numbers, then add
    top = np.dot(a, b)
    # length of each vector: square root of sum of squares
    bottom = np.linalg.norm(a) * np.linalg.norm(b)
    return top / bottom

aarav = ratings.loc["Aarav"].values
for name in ratings.index:
    if name == "Aarav":
        continue
    other = ratings.loc[name].values
    dist = np.linalg.norm(aarav - other)   # the ruler
    cos = cosine(aarav, other)             # the compass
    print(name, "distance:", round(dist, 2), "cosine:", round(cos, 3))

Check yourself

1. Why does cosine say Zoya is like Aarav when distance doesn't?

Cosine only looks at direction. Zoya's ratings go up and down in the same places as Aarav's, just smaller. Distance also counts the size of the numbers, so her lower scores look like different taste.

2. Two users rate everything 5 and everything 1. What is their cosine similarity?

1. Both vectors point in exactly the same direction (one is 5 times the other). That's a weakness: neither tells us anything about taste. Mean-centering in week 2 fixes it.

3. Why can't "most popular" ever be personal?

It uses no information about the person asking, so everyone gets the same list. It still works as a baseline for a new user we know nothing about, which is the cold-start problem.
